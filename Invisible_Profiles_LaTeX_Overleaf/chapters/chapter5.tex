\chaptertitlepage{The Swedish Typology}{The structure of late-life experience under universal welfare}
\label{ch:ch5}

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:ch5-overview}

The K-means segmentation of Chapter~3 recovers six archetypes spanning the Swedish over-65 analytical sample. The Swedish typology is not a Nordic counterpart of the Italian inventory of Chapter~4: the two partitions differ in structural shape on three axes, developed in \S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features} and read in Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6} as the empirical signature of the Nordic-universalistic welfare regime.

All centroid coordinates are reported as z-scores within Sweden on the analytical sample ($n = 1{,}787$). Cluster shares are reported as percentages of the analytical sample; see \S\ref{sec:ch3-preprocessing} for the descriptive/predictive interpretation of the partition.

\label{sec:ch5-six-archetypes}

Two raw-scale observations from Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-profiles} anchor the descriptions that follow. First, the Fragile cluster is the only profile with non-trivial functional burden (mobility 3.79, ADL 0.69, IADL 1.54); the remaining five profiles are all in the autonomous range (mobility $\leq 1.55$, ADL $\leq 0.10$). Second, only the Fragile cluster is below 75\% internet use (63.6\%); the other five profiles span 75.1\% to 98.7\%. Both observations have no parallel in the Italian typology and are developed as substantive findings in \S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features}.

\section{Profile descriptions}
\label{sec:ch5-profile-descriptions}

The six archetypes are described below from most fragile to most connected, in the order of Table~\ref{tab:ch5-archetypes}. Standardised values are z-units within Sweden ($\bar{x} = 0$, $\sigma = 1$); raw means in parenthesis where useful.

\label{sec:ch5-fragile}
\textbf{Fragile} (11.5\%; mean age 80.4) is the only archetype with substantial burden on every health and functional dimension (mobility $+1.95$, IADL $+1.79$, ADL $+1.55$, EURO-D $+1.05$) and the lowest subjective signature in the typology (CASP $-1.43$, raw 33.6; loneliness $+1.07$; hope $-1.45$, 80.6\% against $\geq 90\%$ in the non-fragile profiles). Its internet use, 63.6\%, is materially below the other five Swedish profiles (all $\geq 75\%$) yet several times higher than the Italian fragile profiles (8.6\% Fragile Resigned, 17.3\% Fragile Depressed; \S\ref{sec:ch4-profile-descriptions}). The cluster carries the entire age burden, its share rising from 6\% in 65--74 to 37\% in 85+ (\S\ref{sec:ch5-demographics}); the contrast with the two qualitatively distinct Italian fragile profiles is the first distinctive feature (\S\ref{sec:ch5-binary-fragility}).

\label{sec:ch5-five-autonomous}
The remaining 88.5\% partitions across five autonomous profiles that share intact functional health (mobility $\leq 1.55$, ADL $\leq 0.10$, IADL $\leq 0.23$) and high subjective well-being (CASP $\geq 37$, life satisfaction $\geq 8.0$), separating on economic resources and engagement style. \textbf{Connected Wealthy} (30.0\%; mean age 73.1) is the largest archetype and the high-resource, high-digital pole on every dimension (internet 98.5\%, network 4.07, CASP 42.1, loneliness 3.3), the structural analogue of the Italian Connected Active with a larger share. \textbf{Asset Rich} (19.1\%; mean age 77.7) is the older, wealth-anchored variant with intact health, lower digital engagement (internet 75.1\%) and the smallest network (2.77); together with \textbf{Wealthy Digital} (7.7\%; mean age 74.9), a younger well-resourced cluster with high digital adoption (internet 96.4\%, CASP 40.3) and a thin LCA boundary with Connected Wealthy (49.3\% convergence), it forms the two profiles with no Italian counterpart. \textbf{Moderate} (23.3\%; mean age 76.6) is the middle-stratum cluster centred on the Swedish mean (internet 93.8\%, network 3.91), the counterpart of the autonomous middle stratum that in Italy splits into Moderate Isolated and Traditional Social. \textbf{Social Decline} (8.3\%; mean age 72.6) is, despite the canonical label, the youngest and most residual cluster (internet 98.7\%, network 4.14, CASP 40.3, hope 98.0\%), capturing younger, fully digital, socially-integrated individuals distinguished by moderate income rather than declining engagement; the label is retained for cross-wave consistency (Chapter~\ref{ch:ch7}).

\section{Three distinctive features of the Swedish typology}
\label{sec:ch5-distinctive-features}

Three substantive findings distinguish the Swedish partition from the Italian one and carry the cross-country comparison of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6}: the binary structure of Swedish fragility (\S\ref{sec:ch5-binary-fragility}), the universal digital saturation across the autonomous profiles (\S\ref{sec:ch5-digital-saturation}), and the age-driven rotation of the high-resource pole (\S\ref{sec:ch5-age-prosperity-rotation}).

\subsection{Binary fragility versus graduated fragility}
\label{sec:ch5-binary-fragility}

The Swedish typology partitions the over-65 population along a binary fragility line: a single Fragile cluster (11.5\%) carries the entire functional burden, against five autonomous profiles (88.5\%) that share intact health and high subjective well-being. The Italian typology partitions the same population along a graduated fragility line: two qualitatively distinct fragile profiles (Fragile Resigned 13.2\% and Fragile Depressed 26.7\%, jointly 39.9\%) span an active-depression-versus-settled-resignation contrast that drives separate policy responses (mental-health services in the first case, long-term-care provision in the second; \S\ref{sec:ch4-profile-descriptions}).

The two countries place their principal fragile cluster in the same region of the joint functional-affective burden space, but Italy adds a second, qualitatively distinct cluster (Fragile Depressed) with high affective burden without proportionate functional burden, while Sweden produces no analogous second cluster (the cross-country centroid plane is shown for the matched-pair analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-matched-pair-scatter}). The regime reading is developed in \S\ref{sec:ch6-binary-graduated}.

\subsection{Universal digital saturation}
\label{sec:ch5-digital-saturation}

Internet adoption in the five autonomous Swedish profiles ranges from 75.1\% (Asset Rich) to 98.7\% (Social Decline), four of the five above 93\%. The Italian autonomous profiles span a far wider range, from 17.3\% (Fragile Depressed) and 34.1\% (Moderate Isolated) to 65.8\% (Traditional Social) and 74.8\% (Connected Active; \S\ref{sec:ch4-profile-descriptions}). Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-digital} plots the per-profile means against a 75\% reference line: in Sweden only the Fragile cluster lies below it, in Italy no profile reaches it. The Swedish digital floor is the Italian digital ceiling.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/05_sweden/fig_5_B_digital_saturation.png}
  \caption[Internet use by profile, Italy and Sweden]{Internet use by profile, Italy and Sweden. Cluster means on the binary internet-use indicator (SHARE Wave~9). Dashed reference line at 75\%. The substantive point is not that Sweden is more digitally adopted on average, but that digital adoption cuts at the functional-fragility line in Sweden and at the resource line in Italy; the regime reading and product-design implications are developed in \S\ref{sec:ch6-digital-cut}.

\subsection{The age-prosperity rotation}
\label{sec:ch5-age-prosperity-rotation}

The Swedish high-resource pole is not occupied by a single archetype across the late-life trajectory. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-rotation} plots the within-age-band share of the two well-resourced profiles in each country across the 65--74, 75--84, 85+ bands.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/05_sweden/fig_5_C_age_prosperity_rotation.png}
  \caption[Well-resourced profiles by age band, Italy and Sweden]{Share of well-resourced profiles within age band, Italy and Sweden. Italy (left) shows both Connected Active and Traditional Social declining in parallel with age. Sweden (right) shows Connected Wealthy contracting (41\%$\to$7\%) while Asset Rich expands (14\%$\to$31\%): the high-resource pole rotates from a digital-engagement anchor (young-old) to a wealth-accumulation anchor (oldest-old).}
  \label{fig:ch5-rotation}
\end{figure}

In Sweden the Connected Wealthy share declines from 41\% to 7\% across the three bands while the Asset Rich share rises from 14\% to 31\%, crossing over in the 75--84 band: the high-resource pole rotates from a digital-engagement anchor (Connected Wealthy, young-old) to a wealth-accumulation anchor (Asset Rich, oldest-old) as the cohort ages. The Italian typology has no analogue: both well-resourced profiles decline in parallel (Connected Active 31\%$\to$11\%, Traditional Social 9\%$\to$3\%). The regime reading is developed in \S\ref{sec:ch6-age-rotation}.

\section{External validation: compressed variance under universal welfare}
\label{sec:ch5-external-validation}

Life satisfaction (SHARE Wave~9 item AC012, eleven-point scale), administered in a separate questionnaire block from the analytical input set (\S\ref{sec:ch3-subjective-circularity}), provides an external validation outcome. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-lifesat} reports the distribution across the six Swedish archetypes; the F-statistic of a one-way ANOVA is reported in the caption.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/05_sweden/fig_5_4_lifesat_anova_sweden.png}
  \caption[Life satisfaction by profile, Sweden]{Life satisfaction by profile, Sweden. Boxplots of SHARE Wave~9 item AC012 (eleven-point scale, 0--10) by cluster membership; diamonds report cluster means. One-way ANOVA: $F(5, 1779) = 44.3$, $p < .001$. Lifesat is external to the K-means input set. $n = 1{,}785$.}
  \label{fig:ch5-lifesat}
\end{figure}

Mean life satisfaction is 7.42 in Fragile, against 8.06 (Moderate), 8.47 (Wealthy Digital), 8.48 (Asset Rich), 8.51 (Social Decline), and 8.88 (Connected Wealthy). The F-statistic of 44.3 on $(5, 1779)$ degrees of freedom ($p < 0.001$) confirms significant discriminative power but at less than half the Italian magnitude ($F(4, 2183) = 124.6$; \S\ref{sec:ch4-external-validation}). Because $F$ is not comparable across partitions with different cluster counts, the effect size is reported as $\eta^2$: the typology accounts for approximately 19\% of the life-satisfaction variance in Italy against 11\% in Sweden. The non-fragile Swedish clusters span only 0.82 points (8.06--8.88), and even the most adverse Swedish segment (7.42) exceeds the mean of both Italian fragile profiles (6.5 and 7.2). This is the universal-welfare baseline compressing between-cluster variation in subjective wellbeing, consistent with the welfare-regime literature on the de-commodification of late-life well-being under universal-access institutions \citep{espingandersen1990}.

\section{Demographic distributions across profiles}
\label{sec:ch5-demographics}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-demographics} reports profile composition by gender and age band, each as a 100\% stacked bar.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/05_sweden/fig_5_5_demographics_sweden.png}
  \caption[Profile composition by gender and age, Sweden]{Profile composition by gender and age band, Sweden. Each column shows the percentage of the demographic group falling in each archetype; columns sum to 100\%. Panel (b) shows the age-prosperity rotation discussed in \S\ref{sec:ch5-age-prosperity-rotation}. Legend on panel (b) applies to both panels.}
  \label{fig:ch5-demographics}
\end{figure}

A gender gradient in late-life socio-economic position is present in Sweden but materially attenuated relative to Italy. The two profiles outside the high-resource and middle strata, Fragile (low functional health) and Social Decline (moderate income, not declining engagement), together account for 23\% of Swedish women (13.9\% Fragile + 9.3\% Social Decline) against 16\% of men (8.8\% + 7.2\%), a 7-percentage-point gap against the 15-percentage-point gap observed in Italy (\S\ref{sec:ch4-demographics}). The high-resource profiles (Asset Rich, Wealthy Digital, Connected Wealthy) account for 60\% of men against 54\% of women, again a smaller asymmetry than in Italy. The reading is consistent with the welfare-regime literature on the de-familisation of elderly care in the Nordic countries \citep{anttonen1996}: where institutional care provision is universal, the gender gap in late-life socio-economic outcomes is partially absorbed by public-service substitution rather than amplified by intra-household care obligations.

\section{The absence of the Isolation Paradox}
\label{sec:ch5-no-paradox}

The Italian typology of Chapter~4 identifies the Isolation Paradox as a country-specific configuration in which a substantial segment (Moderate Isolated, 26.9\% of the Italian analytical sample) is functionally and economically autonomous but socially and digitally disconnected (\S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-paradox}). No Swedish archetype reproduces this diagnostic position: the Swedish Moderate cluster, which occupies the corresponding middle-stratum position by health and resources, is instead socially integrated at or above the population mean (network 3.91, internet 93.8\%), and the four other autonomous profiles more strongly still. The Hungarian-matched confirmation of the absent Swedish twin and the healthcare-utilisation quantification across the matched pairs are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6}.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:ch5-synthesis}

The Swedish typology partitions the over-65 population into six archetypes whose structural shape differs systematically from the Italian one. Three findings carry the chapter as the empirical signature of the Nordic-universalistic welfare regime: the binary rather than graduated structure of Swedish fragility, the universal digital saturation that cuts at the functional-fragility line rather than the resource line, and the age-driven rotation of the high-resource pole from a digital-engagement to a wealth-accumulation anchor, with no analogous rotation in Italy. External validation against life satisfaction is significant ($F(5, 1779) = 44.3$, $p < 0.001$) at less than half the Italian magnitude, and the Swedish typology contains no counterpart of the Italian Moderate Isolated configuration; the matched-pair cross-country comparison is developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6}.
