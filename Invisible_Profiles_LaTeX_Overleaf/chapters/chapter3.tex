\section{Empirical strategy}
\label{sec:ch3-empirical-strategy}

The empirical strategy of this thesis rests on three commitments. The first is methodological: the multidimensional structure of late-life experience is recovered from a data-driven segmentation operating on a harmonised analytical set, rather than from a manual partition of cross-tabulations. The second is comparative: two segmentations are estimated independently in Italy and Sweden --- the leading Mediterranean-familistic and Nordic-universalistic cases --- and compared on structurally meaningful axes. The third is conceptual: the analytical set includes both objective dimensions (health, economic resources, digital and social integration, cognitive function) and subjective dimensions (CASP-12, EURO-D, loneliness, hope for the future, expected survival), with the latter entering the segmentation as constituent inputs rather than as external validation outcomes.

This chapter sets out the operational implementation. \S\ref{sec:ch3-data} describes the data and the analytical sample. \S\ref{sec:ch3-variables} lists the variable inventory across the five dimensions. \S\ref{sec:ch3-preprocessing} documents the preprocessing pipeline. \S\ref{sec:ch3-fa} reports the factor-analytic structure of the harmonised input space. \S\ref{sec:ch3-kmeans} details K-means estimation and the choice of $k$. \S\ref{sec:ch3-lca} reports the LCA triangulation. \S\ref{sec:ch3-subjective-commitment} articulates the methodological commitment to subjective dimensions as inputs and reports the empirical sensitivity that justifies it. \S\ref{sec:ch3-reproducibility} documents reproducibility. Internal validity diagnostics, multi-algorithm comparison, and cross-wave panel stability are reported in Chapter~7.

\section{Data and analytical sample}
\label{sec:ch3-data}

The Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) is a multidisciplinary, cross-national panel of individuals aged 50 and older resident in 28 European countries \citep{borschsupan2013}, harmonised ex ante on questionnaire content and ex post on variable definitions. Wave 9 was fielded between June 2021 and August 2022, covering the period that follows the most acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. The wave is the most recent fully released SHARE wave at the time of writing. The pre-pandemic Wave 8 (2019--2020) is used in \S7.6 as the comparator for the cross-wave robustness analysis.

Italy and Sweden are selected on theoretical grounds. The two countries occupy opposite ends of \citet{espingandersen1990} typology of welfare regimes --- Italy as the leading Mediterranean-familistic case (high reliance on intra-family care provision, fragmented social-protection system, predominance of cash transfers in elderly care) and Sweden as the prototypical Nordic-universalistic case (extensive public long-term care, dense community-based social services, elderly-care provision largely de-familised). The two countries also differ markedly in digital adoption among older adults: Eurostat 2022 reports 51\% internet use in the past three months for Italian residents aged 65--74, against 96\% in Sweden.

The analytical sample is restricted to respondents aged 65 or older at the date of interview, with proxy interviews excluded. The SHARE multiple-imputation procedure is applied to income, wealth and pension variables, and the analytical sample uses the first imputation (implicat = 1). Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-sample-flow} reports the sample derivation. The Italian SHARE Wave 9 release contains 5,601 respondents; restriction to the over-65 population reduces the sample to 2,399, listwise deletion on the 31 analytical variables retains 2,378 (a 0.9\% loss), and Mahalanobis-distance outlier screening at $p = 0.001$ removes no further respondents. The Swedish release contains 3,405 respondents; the corresponding sequence yields 2,202, then 2,045 (7.1\% listwise loss), then 1,787 (a further 12.6\% Mahalanobis loss). The asymmetry reflects the higher dimensionality of the Swedish active variable set and a wider tail of the Swedish multivariate distribution in the joint digital-economic subspace; Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-mahalanobis} visualises this asymmetry.

\section{Variable inventory}
\label{sec:ch3-variables}

Thirty-one analytical variables span five conceptual dimensions, following the SHARE conceptual framework on the multidimensional measurement of late-life experience \citep{wahrendorfsiegrist2010, jivraj2020}. Where multiple SHARE items operationalise the same construct (for example, mobility limitations, ADL and IADL for functional health), all candidate items are retained and dimensionality reduction is delegated to the preprocessing and to the clustering algorithm itself.

The physical and functional health block (8 items) aggregates self-rated health (SPHUS), the count of chronic conditions (CHRONIC), basic and instrumental activities of daily living limitations (ADL, IADL), mobility limitations, body mass index, physical inactivity, and the EURO-D depressive-symptoms score. The economic block (5 items) contains log-transformed household income (log\_thinc), log-transformed household net worth (log\_hnetw), individual annual pension income (ypen1), home-ownership status, and self-reported financial distress (FDISTRESS). Income, wealth and pension are imputed via the SHARE multiple-imputation procedure. The digital and social block (10 items) contains a binary internet-use indicator, network size (sn\_size\_w9), a social-integration index, three cultural-engagement binary indicators (ac035d1, ac035d5, ac035d8), and indicators on receipt of help (sp002\_) and provision of help to grandchildren (sp008\_); two further cultural items (ac035d4, ac035d7) have zero variance in Italy and are dropped at variance screening. The cognitive block (3 items) contains verbal fluency (animal-naming), the ten-word recall test (memory), and an orientation-in-time task; cognitive items require CAPI administration and are unavailable for the CATI sub-sample, accounting for the higher Swedish listwise loss reported in \S\ref{sec:ch3-data}. The subjective block (5 items) contains CASP-12 quality of life \citep{hyde2003}, UCLA-3 loneliness \citep{hughes2004}, a binary indicator on hope for the future, four-point interest in the surrounding world, and self-rated probability of survival to a target age.

\section{Preprocessing pipeline}
\label{sec:ch3-preprocessing}

The pipeline operates in five stages: sample selection, variable selection, listwise deletion plus Mahalanobis screening, scale harmonisation by rank transformation followed by z-standardisation, and clustering estimation. Each stage is implemented as a numbered R script with fixed seed 42.

Listwise deletion is applied at the variable level. SHARE imputes income, wealth and pension ex ante \citep{christelis2011}, so the principal source of listwise loss is non-response on cognitive and subjective items. Multiple imputation is not used at the segmentation stage because the K-means objective function is not invariant to imputation noise. After listwise deletion, the sample is screened for multivariate outliers using Mahalanobis distance, with respondents above the chi-squared critical value at $p = 0.001$ excluded; this removes no Italian respondents and 258 Swedish respondents (12.6\%).

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/03_data_methods/fig_mahalanobis_distribution.png}
  \caption{Mahalanobis distance distribution, Italy and Sweden. Density of Mahalanobis distances on the standardised analytical matrix; the dashed line marks the chi-squared critical value at $p = 0.001$. Italy retains all respondents; Sweden loses 12.6\% in the right tail.}
  \label{fig:ch3-mahalanobis}
\end{figure}

Continuous and ordinal variables are then rank-transformed within each country, mapping the original distribution onto a uniform rank distribution; binary variables are passed through unchanged. The rank-transformed and binary variables are z-standardised within each country, so that each variable enters K-means with mean zero and unit variance. Standardisation is performed at the country level rather than on the pooled sample, in order to preserve the within-country interpretation of the centroids: a respondent classified as ``Connected Active'' in Italy is so classified relative to the Italian distribution, and is then compared in Chapter~6 with the analogous ``Connected Wealthy'' archetype in Sweden defined relative to the Swedish distribution.

\section{Factor structure of the harmonised input space}
\label{sec:ch3-fa}

Before estimating the K-means partition, the preprocessed input space is examined through Principal Axis factor analysis. The purpose is twofold: a sanity check on the suitability of the input matrix for dimensionality-reduction methods, and a low-dimensional representation that supports the substantive interpretation of the cluster solution.

The Kaiser--Meyer--Olkin (KMO) measure is 0.832 in Italy and 0.827 in Sweden, both well above the 0.700 threshold conventionally taken as ``good'' adequacy \citep{kaiser1974}; the Bartlett test of sphericity rejects the null in both countries ($p < 0.001$). The number of factors is selected by joint reading of three criteria: parallel analysis \citep{horn1965}, BIC for $k$ between 2 and 8, and substantive interpretability of the rotated loadings. The country-level BIC optima fall at $k = 7$ (Italy) and $k = 4$ (Sweden); the parallel-analysis criterion suggests $k = 6$ (Italy) and $k = 5$ (Sweden); substantive interpretability is highest at $k = 6$ in both countries. The six-factor solution is therefore retained on cross-country-comparability grounds, despite being conservative relative to the BIC optimum in Italy and slightly aggressive in Sweden (RMSEA increase of approximately 0.005 in Italy and 0.010 in Sweden relative to the BIC-optimal solution). Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-fa-fit} reports the four fit indices for $k$ between 4 and 8.

  \label{fig:ch3-fa-fit}

Loadings are rotated by varimax. Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-fa-loadings} reports the Italian solution. The six factors carry interpretable substantive labels: PA1 --- physical and functional fragility (loadings $\geq 0.50$ on self-rated health, IADL, mobility, ADL, chronic conditions); PA5 --- subjective burden (EURO-D 0.73, loneliness 0.55); PA2 --- quality-of-life and engagement (CASP-12 at $-0.64$ indicating that the factor captures the ``low quality of life'' pole, plus internet use and educational participation); PA3 --- social integration (social\_integration 0.95, network size 0.68); PA4 --- household income (log\_thinc 0.87); PA6 --- household wealth (log\_hnetw 0.81, pension 0.93, home-ownership 0.55). The six factors jointly explain 38.4\% of the variance in the 29-variable Italian active set.

A residual diagnostic concerns the social-integration index, which produces a Heywood case in the Italian PA extraction (loading 1.11, communality 1.38) --- values that exceed the unit-variance bound and signal that the variable is empirically over-determined within the social factor (PA3). The Heywood case does not invalidate the factor structure but is reported here in line with the conventions of the factor-analytic literature: the social\_integration index, by construction a composite of social-engagement items, captures essentially the same latent dimension as network size, and the choice to retain the index reflects the preservation of the SHARE-conceptual encoding.

K-means estimation operates on the standardised analytical matrix directly, not on the EFA-extracted factor scores. The factor analysis is therefore an interpretative companion to the segmentation, not an intermediate dimensionality-reduction step.

\section{K-means clustering: estimation and choice of \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}}
\label{sec:ch3-kmeans}

K-means with squared-Euclidean distance is the principal segmentation algorithm, implemented with the Hartigan--Wong update rule \citep{hartiganwong1979} on the country-specific standardised matrix. The estimation is run with 100 random initial-centroid configurations and a fixed seed (42); the configuration with the lowest within-cluster sum of squares is retained. K-means is preferred to agglomerative alternatives (Ward, complete, average) on the basis of higher within-cluster $R^2$ and silhouette indices reported in \S7.3.

The number of clusters $k$ is selected on three criteria. The within-cluster sum of squares scree ($k$ between 2 and 12) places the elbow at $k = 5$ (Italy) and $k = 6$ (Sweden). The Duda--Hart pseudo-$T^2$ statistic on the Ward dendrogram \citep{dudahartstork2001} peaks independently at the same values. Substantive interpretability is decisive at the boundary cases: at $k = 4$ the Italian fragile stratum collapses into a single archetype that obscures a substantively important contrast between ``Resigned'' and ``Depressed'' fragile sub-types (\S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-resigned}, \S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-depressed}); at $k = 6$ the Italian middle stratum splits into two unstable profiles. Table~\ref{tab:ch3-internal-validity} reports the internal validity at the chosen $k$.

The mean silhouette values of 0.069 (Italy) and 0.060 (Sweden) are low in absolute terms --- below the 0.250 threshold conventionally taken as indicative of a cohesive cluster structure \citep{kaufman1990}. This is a routine consequence of clustering on a high-dimensional input matrix spanning multiple FA-correlated subspaces, in which Euclidean compactness is intrinsically limited and within-cluster heterogeneity remains material along the dimensions on which the partition does not separate. The Duda--Hart pseudo-$T^2$ peak and the substantive interpretability of the resulting profiles therefore carry decisive weight in the choice of $k$, with the full silhouette decomposition by cluster reported in Chapter~7.

\section{Latent Class Analysis triangulation}
\label{sec:ch3-lca}

The K-means hard assignment is triangulated by Latent Class Analysis fitted on a tertilised representation of the same input variables. Continuous and ordinal variables are partitioned at country-level tertile cut-points into a three-category ordinal variable; binary variables are passed through unchanged. The LCA is fitted by the EM algorithm \citep{vermuntmagidson2002} with 100 random starts. The number of latent classes is held fixed at the K-means $k$ for each country, so the two partitions are directly comparable.

Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-lca-convergence} reports the agreement between the K-means hard assignment and the LCA modal-class assignment by profile. Italian profiles converge between 55\% (Moderate Isolated) and 96\% (Fragile Resigned); Swedish profiles between 49\% (Wealthy Digital) and 87\% (Connected Wealthy). The four corner archetypes converge well above the 50\% chance line; the lower convergence on the middle-stratum profiles localises the regions where the cluster boundary is intrinsically fuzzy. The two methods therefore validate each other on the well-defined corners and identify the boundary regions where both algorithms struggle \citep{vermuntmagidson2002}.

\section{Methodological commitment: subjective dimensions as constituent inputs}
\label{sec:ch3-subjective-commitment}

A central methodological choice of this thesis is that the five subjective measures listed in \S\ref{sec:ch3-variables} --- CASP-12, EURO-D, loneliness, hope for the future, and self-rated probability of survival --- enter the K-means and LCA stages as inputs on equal footing with the objective health, economic, digital and social variables. They are not held aside for use as external validation outcomes. This section sets out the choice, its theoretical grounding, the empirical evidence that supports it, and a response to the objection that the resulting typology is in some sense circular.

\subsection{The analytic choice}
\label{sec:ch3-subjective-analytic}

A common architecture in segmentation studies of older adults is to cluster on objective indicators and to use subjective dimensions as external validators \citep{wahrendorfsiegrist2010, jivraj2020}. The implicit position is that subjective dimensions are downstream consequences of the objective state. The architecture adopted here takes the opposite view: subjective dimensions are constituent dimensions of the lived experience of aging, with empirical content partially independent of the objective state. A typology that includes subjective measures as inputs cannot, by construction, be used to predict subjective well-being from cluster membership; it can be used to identify groups whose multidimensional configuration of objective and subjective experience is sufficiently coherent to be treated as a recognisable pattern of late-life trajectory.

\subsection{Theoretical grounding}
\label{sec:ch3-subjective-theory}

\citet{lalivedepinay2005} develop late life as a distinct phase whose meaning is shaped jointly by objective resources and subjective interpretation, and explicitly argue against treating one as the cause of the other. \citet{walker2005active}, in his programmatic statement of the active-ageing paradigm, situates subjective control, sense of meaning, and life evaluation as core components of the aging process. The CASP scale \citep{hyde2003} was designed precisely to capture constituent quality-of-life dimensions of older age. In the gerontological-health literature, \citet{idlerbenyamini1997} showed that self-rated health predicts mortality independently of clinically observed health, and \citet{bowling2006} reviews two decades of work concluding that subjective evaluations should be treated as primary indicators of late-life experience rather than as derivative outcomes. The EURO-D depression scale \citep{prince1999} was constructed to provide a multi-country comparable measure of late-life depressive affect contributing to a constituent description of older-adult well-being.

\subsection{Empirical justification: 4D versus 5D sensitivity}
\label{sec:ch3-subjective-empirical}

A direct empirical test re-runs the segmentation pipeline with the subjective block removed, retaining only the four objective dimensions for clustering and holding all other parameters constant. External discriminative power is measured by the F-statistic of a one-way ANOVA of life satisfaction (SHARE item AC012, eleven-point scale) on cluster membership; life satisfaction is external to both specifications. The Adjusted Rand Index between the 5D and 4D partitions measures how much the individual cluster assignments shift.

Internal validity indices increase modestly when the subjective block is dropped --- the routine consequence of clustering on a smaller variable space. The external discriminative power on life satisfaction falls sharply: a 41\% reduction in Italy and a 22\% reduction in Sweden. The ARI between the 4D and 5D partitions is 0.41 in Italy and 0.52 in Sweden --- moderate agreement, meaning roughly half of the individual cluster assignments are sensitive to the inclusion of the subjective block. In Italy the 4D specification collapses the qualitative contrast between Fragile Resigned and Fragile Depressed (\S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-resigned}, \S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-depressed}) into a single fragile cluster, eliminating the affective distinction that the 5D specification preserves. The asymmetry between Italy ($-41\%$) and Sweden ($-22\%$) suggests that the subjective dimension contributes more uniquely to the Italian aging experience than to the Swedish one --- consistent with the substantive finding (Chapter~6) that the cross-country gap on subjective well-being indicators is wider than the gap on objective ones.

\subsection{Response to the circularity objection}
\label{sec:ch3-subjective-circularity}

The objection that the analytical strategy is circular --- that the typology cannot validly be evaluated against subjective outcomes because subjective measures enter the clustering as inputs --- is addressed directly in what follows. Three points are relevant. First, the objection rests on a specific framing of what the typology is for. If the typology is intended to predict subjective well-being from objective state, then circularity is a real problem and the four-dimensional specification is the appropriate analytical strategy. The typology developed here is not intended for that purpose; it is intended to describe coherent multidimensional configurations of late-life experience, and includes all dimensions on equal footing. Second, the discriminative-power gain reported in Table~\ref{tab:ch3-4d-5d-sensitivity} is measured against life satisfaction, an outcome correlated with but not identical to any of the five subjective inputs and administered in a separate questionnaire block. The gain is closer to a test of construct validity than to a tautology. Third, where the typology is applied to genuinely external validation --- pension-system attachment, healthcare utilisation, regional variation, policy take-up --- the validation outcomes are entirely separate from the clustering inputs (\S7.4, \S7.5).

\section{Reproducibility protocol}
\label{sec:ch3-reproducibility}

The full analytical pipeline is implemented in twenty numbered R scripts (00--19) plus two helper modules (var\_labels.R; fig\_paths.R), executed sequentially in a versioned repository under git. The pipeline builds on the analytical scaffold of the Data Analytics and Visualization course \citep{piccarreta2025}. Each script is parameterised by a single seed (42) and re-seeds before each stochastic step. All intermediate and output files are written to a versioned outputs directory and indexed by the script number that generated them. The full pipeline runs in approximately forty minutes on a standard laptop, with R-package versions pinned via renv. A separate web application (\url{https://invisible-profiles.vercel.app}) presents the typology, the cross-country comparison, and the robustness diagnostics in interactive form, regenerated automatically from the analytical outputs.
