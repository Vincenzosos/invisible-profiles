\chaptertitlepage{Cross-Country Comparison}{Welfare regime as the structuring institution of late-life experience}
\label{ch:ch6}

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:ch6-overview}

Chapters~\ref{ch:ch4} and~\ref{ch:ch5} have presented two parallel typologies of the over-65 population obtained through identical pipelines on identical input variables, with the only difference being the country of reference. The two partitions differ on the structural shape of the fragility-and-resources gradient, on the level and meaning of digital adoption, and on the role of age in shaping the high-resource pole. The interpretation pursued throughout the empirical chapters has anticipated that these differences are not artefacts of sampling or modelling choices, but the empirical signature of two distinct welfare regimes operating on otherwise comparable European societies. This chapter develops that interpretation as the central comparative argument of the thesis.

The argument proceeds in five steps. \S\ref{sec:ch6-welfare-regime} sets out the welfare-regime framework and identifies the operational features --- de-familisation of elderly care, universal-access information infrastructure, public versus intra-household provision --- that the comparison brings to the data. \S\ref{sec:ch6-matched-pairs} reports the formal cross-country alignment of the two typologies through a Hungarian-matched analysis of standardised cluster centroids, identifies the four matched pairs that have a structural counterpart in both countries, and isolates the three country-specific profiles (one Italian, two Swedish) that have no structural twin and that carry most of the comparative content. \S\ref{sec:ch6-three-contrasts} synthesises the three structural contrasts already developed within Chapter~\ref{ch:ch5} --- binary versus graduated fragility, the digital cut, and the age-prosperity rotation --- and reads them jointly as the regime signature. \S\ref{sec:ch6-healthcare} reports the cross-country comparison of healthcare utilisation across the four matched pairs and across the unmatched Italian Moderate Isolated profile, completing the empirical case for the Isolation Paradox developed in \S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-paradox}. \S\ref{sec:ch6-silver-economy} draws the implications for the structure of the silver-economy market in the two countries and identifies the design gap that the Italian configuration creates. \S\ref{sec:ch6-synthesis} closes the chapter and prepares the policy and conclusion arguments of Chapter~8.

All centroid coordinates are reported as z-scores within the country of origin to preserve the within-country structural reading of the typologies. Cross-country comparisons of raw indicators are reported on the original scales (percentages for binary indicators, count means for utilisation variables). Sample sizes are $n = 2{,}378$ for Italy and $n = 1{,}787$ for Sweden. Cluster shares are reported as percentages of the respective analytical sample; see \S\ref{sec:ch3-preprocessing} for the descriptive/predictive interpretation. The only inferential claims at the national level developed in this chapter are the scenario-based extrapolations of \S\ref{sec:ch6-time-to-maturity}, which operate on the Eurostat 65--74 cohort time-series and not on the cluster assignments.

\section{Welfare regime as the structuring institution}
\label{sec:ch6-welfare-regime}

The comparative literature on European welfare regimes identifies two configurations that differ systematically on the institutional treatment of elderly care, on the role of the household as a unit of provision, and on the architecture of the public-service infrastructure. The Nordic-universalistic regime, of which Sweden is the canonical case, is characterised by an extensive public-sector elderly-care branch, a high level of de-familisation of care obligations, and a universal-access information and service infrastructure that operates independently of household composition or family proximity \citep{espingandersen1990, anttonen1996, bambra2007}. The Mediterranean-familistic regime, of which Italy is the canonical case, is characterised by a residual public elderly-care branch, a high level of intra-household and intra-family responsibility for elderly support, and a fragmented public-service infrastructure in which formal access depends materially on informal navigation channels \citep{ferrera1996, naldini2008, saraceno2010}. The two regimes have been documented to produce systematically different outcomes on female labour-force participation in the cohorts with elderly care obligations \citep{bettio2004, bonsang2009}, on the gender distribution of late-life poverty risk \citep{ferrera1996}, and on the social distribution of healthcare access \citep{loscalzo2009}.

The empirical content of this thesis adds to that comparative literature a typological reading: the two regimes do not only produce different aggregate outcomes for the over-65 population, they produce different \emph{partitions} of that population along the principal axes of late-life experience. The Italian over-65 population, treated as a unit by the welfare regime that surrounds it, organises itself into five clusters in which the principal latent dimension is the joint resource-and-engagement gradient and in which a country-specific middle-stratum profile (Moderate Isolated) emerges from the interaction of intact autonomy with a thin informational environment. The Swedish over-65 population, treated as a unit by a structurally different welfare regime, organises itself into six clusters in which the principal latent dimension is a binary fragility line and in which the high-resource pole rotates across age bands rather than contracting in aggregate. The thesis claim is that the two partitions are the empirical signature of the two regimes acting on the same demographic substrate.

This chapter develops the claim through three operational arguments. The first is structural: the two typologies have only four matched profile pairs out of seven cluster slots --- four of the five Italian profiles have a Swedish twin (Moderate Isolated does not), and four of the six Swedish profiles have an Italian twin (Asset Rich and Wealthy Digital do not) --- and the three unmatched profiles carry most of the comparative content. The second is dimensional: each of the three substantive findings already established within Chapter~\ref{ch:ch5} (binary versus graduated fragility, the digital cut, the age-prosperity rotation) is the within-typology projection of the same regime contrast onto a different latent dimension. The third is policy-relevant: the cross-country comparison of healthcare utilisation across the four matched pairs and the unmatched Italian Moderate Isolated profile produces a set of country-specific gaps that, in conjunction with the structural reading, identify a design space for the Italian silver economy that the Swedish data quantifies.

\section{Hungarian-matched cross-country pairs}
\label{sec:ch6-matched-pairs}

The two typologies are aligned by the Hungarian assignment algorithm operating on the matrix of pairwise centroid distances between Italian and Swedish profiles in the standardised analytical space \citep{kaufman1990}. The standardised distance is defined on the eleven analytical dimensions of the K-means partition (objective health: mobility, ADL, IADL, chronic conditions, self-rated health; subjective dimensions: EURO-D, loneliness, hope, CASP; resources: log-income, log-wealth) with z-scoring performed within country to preserve the within-country structural reading. The assignment minimises the sum of distances under the constraint that each Italian profile is matched to at most one Swedish profile, with a maximum-distance threshold ($d \leq 2.0$ in standardised units) used to declare a match structurally meaningful. Profiles that exceed the threshold are reported as unmatched.

The procedure returns four matched pairs and three unmatched profiles. Table~\ref{tab:ch6-matched-pairs} reports the matched pairs with their respective sample shares and a one-line characterisation of the structural correspondence. Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-matched-pair-scatter} visualises the cluster centroids of the two typologies on a joint plane defined by physical/functional fragility (mean z-score on objective health and functional dimensions) and subjective burden (mean z-score on the subjective block). The unmatched profiles are: in Italy, the Moderate Isolated cluster (26.9\%; \S\ref{sec:ch4-moderate-isolated}); in Sweden, the Asset Rich cluster (19.1\%; \S\ref{sec:ch5-five-autonomous}) and the Wealthy Digital cluster (7.7\%; \S\ref{sec:ch5-five-autonomous}). The three unmatched profiles together cover 26.9\% of the Italian analytical sample and 26.8\% of the Swedish analytical sample, and they carry the bulk of the comparative content of the chapter.

Two observations on the matched-pair structure are substantively important. First, the matching is not a one-to-one identity: the four pairs differ materially on population share within the matched correspondence. The Connected pair is the largest in both countries (25.4\% Italy, 30.0\% Sweden), with the Swedish share approximately five percentage points above the Italian share, consistent with the deeper diffusion of the digitally-integrated profile under universal welfare. The Socially-oriented pair is the most asymmetric: 7.7\% in Italy against 23.3\% in Sweden, reflecting the absorption into the Swedish Moderate cluster of the middle stratum that splits in Italy into Traditional Social and the unmatched Moderate Isolated. The Declining pair is asymmetric in the opposite direction (26.7\% Italy against 8.3\% Sweden), with the residual character of the Swedish Social Decline cluster (\S\ref{sec:ch5-five-autonomous}) accounting for the size mismatch. The Fragile pair is the only matched pair with comparable size in the two countries (13.2\% Italy, 11.5\% Sweden), confirming that the principal fragility profile is approximately identically distributed across the two welfare regimes despite the substantially different aggregate level of public elderly-care provision.

A caveat applies to the Declining pair specifically. The Hungarian algorithm minimises a centroid distance in the standardised space and is therefore agnostic to the substantive content of the matched profiles. As §\ref{sec:ch5-five-autonomous} documents, the Wave~9 Swedish Social Decline cluster is the residual cluster with the most ambiguous substantive content (youngest archetype, full digital adoption, largest network, EURO-D 2.0). Its assignment to the Italian Fragile Depressed cluster reflects geometric proximity in the joint analytical space rather than a substantive declining-affect-and-engagement parallel. The cross-country gap magnitudes reported on the Declining row of \S\ref{sec:ch6-pair-gaps} (the largest dental gap and the largest forgone-care gap of the four matched pairs) should therefore be read as the upper-bound of the within-pair Italy--Sweden differential under a structural matching that operates on the analytical centroid and not as a regime-attributable gap on a substantively homogeneous pair.

Second, the unmatched profiles map directly onto the regime contrast. The single Italian unmatched profile (Moderate Isolated) is the configuration that the welfare regime literature predicts as the residual of a familistic regime that does not provide a substitute infrastructure for individuals not embedded in dense family networks: intact autonomy, thin social and digital engagement, and below-average healthcare utilisation. The two Swedish unmatched profiles (Asset Rich and Wealthy Digital) are the configurations that the welfare regime literature predicts as the residual of a universalistic regime that allows the high-resource pole to differentiate by age and by engagement style without contracting: a wealth-anchored profile of the oldest-old (Asset Rich, mean age 77.7) and a digitally-anchored profile of the well-resourced young-old (Wealthy Digital, mean age 74.9). The three unmatched profiles together are not a residual category of the modelling exercise; they are the empirical content of the regime contrast.

\section{Three structural contrasts as the regime signature}
\label{sec:ch6-three-contrasts}

The three substantive findings developed in \S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features} are not three independent contrasts. They are three projections of the same regime distinction onto three different latent dimensions of late-life experience. This section reads them jointly.

\subsection{Binary versus graduated fragility}
\label{sec:ch6-binary-graduated}

The Swedish typology partitions the over-65 population along a binary fragility line (a single Fragile cluster) against the Italian graduated structure of two distinct fragile profiles (\S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features}). Read as a regime signature, the contrast is operational. In a regime that absorbs the functional burden of advanced old age into a public elderly-care branch with universal coverage \citep{anttonen1996, bonsang2009}, the institutional infrastructure produces a single targetable segment: the Fragile cluster. Affective deficits within the autonomous profiles do not separate into a second fragility cluster because they do not align with a corresponding functional or institutional cleavage; the universal infrastructure absorbs them at the margin. In a regime that distributes the same burden across the household and the family \citep{ferrera1996, saraceno2010, naldini2008}, the institutional infrastructure produces multiple distinguishable segments because the burden is not absorbed institutionally and its operational characteristics (functional autonomy, affective trajectory, family proximity) co-vary with the household-level configuration in which the individual is embedded. The Italian graduated structure is what the regime produces; the Swedish binary structure is what the regime absorbs.

\subsection{The digital cut: functional line versus resource line}
\label{sec:ch6-digital-cut}

The same internet-use indicator marks two structurally different cleavages: in Sweden it cuts at the functional-fragility line; in Italy it tracks the resource gradient (\S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features}). Read as a regime signature, the contrast is interpretive. In a regime where the public-service infrastructure operates through a universal-access digital channel and where the individual's relation to the welfare state does not require informal mediation \citep{anttonen1996, bambra2007}, the digital indicator measures the extent to which the individual remains within the autonomous range of the typology: the near-universal adoption across all profiles except the principal fragility cluster is the operational footprint of the universal channel. In a regime where the public-service infrastructure operates through fragmented channels and where the individual's relation to the welfare state is mediated by family and informal networks \citep{ferrera1996, loscalzo2009}, the digital indicator measures the cumulative socio-economic and informational endowment of the individual: the wide gap between the lowest- and highest-adoption clusters is the operational footprint of the resource gradient. The same variable measures different things because the regimes produce different relations between the individual and the digital channel.

\subsection{The age-prosperity rotation}
\label{sec:ch6-age-rotation}

The Italian high-resource pole declines in parallel with age, while the Swedish pole rotates --- Connected Wealthy contracting and Asset Rich expanding across the age bands (\S\ref{sec:ch5-distinctive-features}). The reading of the rotation as a regime signature ties to the institutional infrastructure that supports late-life autonomy. In a regime where the public elderly-care branch absorbs the functional burden of advanced old age \citep{anttonen1996, bonsang2009}, the asset-rich household can remain autonomous into the 85+ band without family-mediated care provision: the Asset Rich profile is the late-life configuration that the universal infrastructure permits. In a regime where elderly care is delivered through intra-household and intra-family channels \citep{ferrera1996, saraceno2010, naldini2008}, the asset-rich older household does not have an analogous institutional substitute, and the late-life trajectory contracts the high-resource pole into the fragile region rather than rotating it. The Italian parallel decline is the late-life trajectory under familistic absorption; the Swedish rotation is the late-life trajectory under universalistic absorption.

\section{Healthcare utilisation across matched pairs}
\label{sec:ch6-healthcare}

The cross-country comparison of healthcare utilisation across the four matched pairs and the unmatched Italian Moderate Isolated profile completes the comparative reading. The analysis is reported on the four indicators shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-healthcare-gap} --- dental-care attendance over the twelve months preceding interview, forgone care for cost reasons over the same period, specialist contacts over the same period, and CASP-12 quality of life (the last as a cross-country comparator of subjective wellbeing rather than of utilisation) --- with cluster means and Welch's $t$-test gaps with 95\% confidence intervals on the standardised analytical sample.

\subsection{Country-aggregate gaps}
\label{sec:ch6-aggregate-gaps}

At the country-aggregate level, healthcare utilisation reports a consistent pattern: Italian respondents report more doctor visits (approximately 7.0 per year against a Swedish mean of 5.5) and comparable specialist contacts (2.1 against 2.1), but materially higher rates of forgone care for cost reasons (10.1\% against 2.7\%) and substantially lower dental-care attendance (30.4\% against 83.8\%). These are country-aggregate figures; doctor-contact volume in particular is reported here as aggregate context and is not among the four matched-pair panels of Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-healthcare-gap}. The Italian system delivers higher generalist-contact volume at the aggregate but no comparable advantage on the navigation-intensive specialist channel, and it produces gaps in coverage at the population margin \citep{loscalzo2009}; the Swedish system delivers comparable specialist volume with a substantially flatter access profile on the cost-sensitive and preventive channels, consistent with the universal-access infrastructure of the Nordic regime \citep{anttonen1996, bambra2007}. The specialist parity is itself consistent with the Isolation Paradox reading, in which the specialist --- the navigation-intensive channel --- is precisely where Italian contact volume confers no advantage (\S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-paradox}).

\subsection{Matched-pair gaps}
\label{sec:ch6-pair-gaps}

The matched-pair analysis isolates the cross-country gaps within structurally comparable profiles; all gaps are reported in percentage points (pp) on the corresponding raw indicator. The Fragile pair (IT Fragile Resigned vs SE Fragile) reports an Italy--Sweden dental gap of 55~pp (raw 19.8\% against 74.6\%, 95\% CI [48, 62]~pp), a forgone-care gap of $-7$~pp (15.2\% against 7.8\%, with the negative sign indicating that Sweden has \emph{less} unmet need) and an internet-use gap of 55~pp (8.6\% against 63.6\%). The Connected pair (IT Connected Active vs SE Connected Wealthy) reports a dental gap of 39~pp (49.3\% against 88.0\%, 95\% CI [34, 44]~pp), a forgone-care gap of $-3$~pp (3.8\% against 0.9\%) and an internet-use gap of 24~pp (74.8\% against 98.5\%). The Socially-oriented pair (IT Traditional Social vs SE Moderate) reports a dental gap of 39~pp (47.8\% against 86.6\%) and a forgone-care gap of $-4$~pp (5.4\% against 1.0\%). The Declining pair (IT Fragile Depressed vs SE Social Decline) reports the largest dental gap (60~pp, 22.0\% against 82.4\%) and the largest forgone-care gap ($-16$~pp, 19.2\% against 3.4\%), consistent with the residual character of the Swedish Social Decline cluster, whose substantive content is closer to a young-old digitally-integrated profile than to a fragile one (\S\ref{sec:ch5-five-autonomous}).

The matched-pair gaps are systematic and substantively important. Across all four pairs, Italian profiles report lower dental-care attendance and higher forgone-care prevalence than their Swedish twins, with gap magnitudes that scale with the substantive content of the pair: largest in the Declining and Fragile pairs (60 and 55 percentage points on dental attendance respectively), smaller in the Connected and Socially-oriented pairs (39 percentage points on dental attendance in both). Figure~\ref{fig:ch6-healthcare-gap} reports the country-mean values on the four healthcare indicators for each matched pair side by side. The pattern indicates that the cross-country gap is not a uniform shift but a structurally graduated one, with the gap widening at the lower end of the resource-and-engagement gradient. The configuration is consistent with a welfare-regime reading in which the Italian system produces healthcare-access deficits that are structurally co-located with the fragile and declining segments of the population.

\subsection{The Isolation Paradox in cross-country perspective}
\label{sec:ch6-isolation-paradox-comparative}

The Italian Moderate Isolated cluster (26.9\% of the Italian analytical sample) is the unmatched profile in the typology and is the central population segment of the Isolation Paradox developed in \S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-paradox}. The cluster reports the lowest healthcare utilisation among the five Italian archetypes (4.61 doctor visits per year against a country mean of 7.0; 1.08 specialist contacts against 2.1; 5.8\% forgone care against 10.1\%) despite having intact health and economic resources comparable to the Traditional Social cluster (\S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-empirical}). The cross-country comparison sharpens the reading: the Swedish typology contains no profile with the Moderate Isolated diagnostic position, and the Hungarian-matching algorithm reports no Swedish twin within the structural distance threshold. The comparative reading is that the Italian Moderate Isolated configuration is not a clinical or behavioural category that exists across welfare regimes; it is a regime-specific category that emerges where the public-service infrastructure does not provide a substitute baseline for individuals not embedded in dense informal networks \citep{ferrera1996, loscalzo2009, saraceno2010}.

The substantive implication for healthcare-system design is direct. The Moderate Isolated configuration does not present in the existing Italian welfare branches: not in long-term-care, where the cluster signature would not satisfy the eligibility criteria on functional autonomy; not in income-support, where it would not satisfy the eligibility criteria on resources; not in mental-health, where it does not present elevated affective scores. The segment is invisible to the operational categories of the regime that produces it. The Swedish counterpart of this configuration does not exist because the regime that produces the Swedish typology integrates the corresponding population segment into the universal-access infrastructure at the baseline. The Italian-only character of the configuration is the empirical signature of the regime contrast on the dimension of social and informational mediation.

\section{Implications for silver-economy market structure}
\label{sec:ch6-silver-economy}

The cross-country comparison developed in \S\ref{sec:ch6-three-contrasts} and \S\ref{sec:ch6-healthcare} has direct implications for the structure of the silver-economy market in the two countries. The implications are not symmetric: the Swedish typology delivers a market that is structurally accessible to direct-to-consumer digital products across most of the Swedish analytical sample, while the Italian typology delivers a market that is structurally segmented into addressable and non-addressable cluster groups on the basis of dimensions that are themselves the empirical signature of the regime.

\subsection{Addressability under the two typologies}
\label{sec:ch6-addressability}

In Sweden, direct-to-consumer digital products are addressable across the 88.5\% of the Swedish analytical sample classified into the five autonomous profiles, with intermediated or alternative-channel designs required only for the 11.5\% Fragile cluster. The digital floor across the autonomous profiles is high enough that internet-mediated services do not face a substantial penetration constraint within the autonomous range of the typology, and the asset-rich households that occupy the high-resource pole in advanced old age (Asset Rich, 19.1\%) provide a wealth-anchored autonomous segment whose product preferences differ from the digital-engagement-anchored Connected Wealthy segment but whose addressability through digital channels is preserved. The Swedish silver-economy market is structurally a single market with internal differentiation by engagement style and resource configuration.

In Italy, the same direct-to-consumer digital approach is addressable for approximately one third of the analytical sample (Connected Active 25.4\% + Traditional Social 7.7\% = 33.1\%), with the remaining two thirds requiring fundamentally different design logics. Within this two-thirds, the Fragile Resigned segment (13.2\%) requires intermediated designs through family or institutional channels (8.6\% internet use; \S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-resigned}), the Fragile Depressed segment (26.7\%) requires affect-aware designs that address the binding behavioural constraint on adoption (17.3\% internet use; \S\ref{sec:ch4-fragile-depressed}), and the Moderate Isolated segment (26.9\%) requires social-mediation designs that bridge the absence of dense informal channels in the configuration (34.1\% internet use; \S\ref{sec:ch4-moderate-isolated}). The Italian silver-economy market is structurally four markets with materially different design logics, of which only one is addressable through direct-to-consumer digital channels at scale.

\subsection{The product-design gap}
\label{sec:ch6-product-design-gap}

The substantive case for innovation in the Italian silver economy is most direct on the Moderate Isolated segment. The segment combines intact autonomy with non-trivial digital adoption (34.1\%), thin social and informational mediation, and systematic disengagement from the formal healthcare system (\S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-healthcare}). It is not addressable by direct-to-consumer digital products in the same way as the Connected Active segment, because the binding constraint is not the absence of a digital channel but the absence of a social-and-informational mediation layer that would route the available digital channel towards healthcare and service utilisation. The Swedish data makes explicit the magnitude of the corresponding gap in qualitative terms: across the four matched pairs, the Swedish dental-attendance rates are higher than the Italian rates by 39 to 60 percentage points (\S\ref{sec:ch6-pair-gaps}), with the largest gaps concentrated in the Declining and Fragile pairs. The gap is descriptive of the within-sample cluster centroids; translating it into a market-size figure for the Italian over-65 population would require an inferential step --- assignment-probability weighting from the descriptive K-means partition to the national population, a partial-conversion rate from gap-closure individuals to private-channel market access, and a per-individual private-premium assumption \citep{rodrigues2023} --- that this thesis does not develop. The substantive content of the gap is therefore stated qualitatively: the cross-country dental-attendance shortfall on the four matched pairs is the empirical counterpart of the absent institutional infrastructure of preventive-care navigation, and it carries the first-order operational implication for the Italian silver-economy design problem.

The complementary case on the unmatched Moderate Isolated segment is structural rather than directly comparable, because the segment has no Swedish reference: a quarter of the Italian sample with intact autonomy and non-trivial digital adoption, systematically disengaged from the formal healthcare system and from the cultural and social channels of late-life service mediation, is a first-order policy and market reality at the level of the cluster centroid. The product-design candidate is the combination of a digital channel with a social-mediation layer (peer-network platforms, community-based digital intermediaries, family-network-augmented service interfaces) that would substitute for the absent informal infrastructure in the Italian configuration. The Swedish typology does not contain a precedent for the design because the regime that produces the Swedish typology does not require it.

\subsection{Time to maturity of the digital channel}
\label{sec:ch6-time-to-maturity}

The Italian internet-use cohort 65--74 is reported at 65.6\% in 2024 \citep{eurostat2024}, against a Swedish 65--74 reference of approximately 96\% on the same Eurostat indicator \citep{eurostat2022}. A simple linear extrapolation calibrated on the Italian time-series (60.4\% in 2023, a one-year gain of 5.2 percentage points) under three rate scenarios (1.0, 3.0 and 5.2 percentage points per year, the upper bound corresponding to the post-pandemic trend) implies that the Italian cohort would close the 30-percentage-point gap to the Swedish reference in approximately 30, 10 and 6 years respectively. The extrapolation is illustrative rather than predictive: digital-adoption curves are bounded and decelerate near saturation, so the linear figure is best read as a lower bound on the time to convergence. The substantive reading is robust to this caveat: the digital divide between the Italian and Swedish typologies is not a transitory phenomenon that demographic replacement closes within a product cycle --- even under the post-pandemic acceleration scenario the gap persists for the better part of a decade, and under the central scenario for a decade or more. The implication for the silver-economy product roadmap is that designs assuming the digital channel as a primary substrate of service delivery face a materially different addressability profile in Italy than in Sweden over the medium-term horizon.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:ch6-synthesis}

The two typologies are partitions of populations whose comparative welfare-regime literature predicts systematically different institutional treatment of elderly care, household responsibility and service infrastructure. Only four of the seven cluster slots have a structural twin across countries (\S\ref{sec:ch6-matched-pairs}); the three unmatched profiles --- Moderate Isolated in Italy, Asset Rich and Wealthy Digital in Sweden --- carry the empirical content of the regime contrast. The three structural contrasts of \S\ref{sec:ch6-three-contrasts} project the regime distinction onto three latent dimensions in a coherent pattern (binary versus graduated fragility, the digital cut at the functional line versus the resource line, the high-resource rotation versus parallel decline), and the healthcare-utilisation analysis of \S\ref{sec:ch6-healthcare} confirms that the regime contrast both scales graduated within-pair gaps and produces a country-only configuration that the Italian welfare branches do not currently address. The silver-economy implications follow directly: the Italian market is structurally different rather than a smaller version of the Swedish one, with the dominant innovation case being the construction of a social-mediation layer that the Italian regime does not provide (\S\ref{sec:ch6-silver-economy}).
