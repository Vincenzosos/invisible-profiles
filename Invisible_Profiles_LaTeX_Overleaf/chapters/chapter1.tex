\chaptertitlepage{Introduction}{Late-life heterogeneity, welfare regimes, and the silver-economy design problem}
\label{ch:ch1}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:ch1-motivation}

Roughly one in four Italians and one in five Swedes are now aged 65 or older \citep{istat2024, scb2024}, and the European silver economy built around them has not yet solved its design problem. The over-65 populations of Europe are larger, longer-lived, more economically autonomous, and internally more heterogeneous than at any earlier moment in the demographic record; policy responses and product strategies, however, still tend to treat those populations as a single demographic block defined externally by age and income. This thesis argues that beneath the apparent demographic uniformity, the same multidimensional segmentation pipeline applied to comparable European data returns systematically different typologies depending on the welfare regime that surrounds the population it serves.

Italy and Sweden are at advanced stages of the same demographic transition, yet the institutional infrastructure that surrounds the late-life condition is structurally different in the two cases. The Italian welfare state delivers elderly support through an architecture in which the household and the family are primary loci of provision; the Swedish welfare state delivers the same support through an architecture in which the public sector is the primary locus and the family is a complementary one. The comparative welfare-state literature has documented the contrast --- Mediterranean-familistic versus Nordic-universalistic --- at the aggregate level on labour-market participation, intergenerational transfers, gender equality, and healthcare access.

The hypothesis pursued in this thesis is that the regime contrast also operates at the typological level: the over-65 populations of the two countries partition into archetypes whose structural shape, internal balance between objective and subjective dimensions, and distributional positioning along age and resource gradients are systematically different in ways that reflect the institutional treatment of the population. The substantive payoff of the hypothesis is operational. A market in which the bulk of the older population is digitally autonomous and integrated into a universal-access infrastructure is structurally different from one in which only a minority is reachable through direct digital channels while the majority requires social-mediation, family-augmented intermediaries, and affect-aware adoption channels. The typological reading developed here is therefore relevant not only to the comparative welfare-regime literature but to the design of European silver-economy products and policies.

\section{Research question}
\label{sec:ch1-question}

The thesis pursues one principal research question and three subsidiary ones.

The principal question is whether the over-65 populations of Italy and Sweden --- surveyed under a comparable instrument (SHARE Wave~9) and analysed through an identical multidimensional segmentation pipeline --- partition into typologies whose structural differences are systematic enough and large enough to be read as the institutional signature of the welfare-regime contrast between the two countries.

The first subsidiary question is whether the inclusion of the subjective block of late-life experience (subjective wellbeing, loneliness, hope, interest, expected survival) as constituent input to the partition delivers a typological gain over the alternative architecture that uses the subjective measures as external validators.

The second subsidiary question is whether the typological partition recovered in each country contains country-specific archetypes whose configuration is not replicated in the other country and that carry the bulk of the comparative content.

The third subsidiary question is whether the typological reading delivers operational implications for the structure of the silver-economy market that the alternative country-aggregate reading does not.

The four questions are addressed empirically in Chapters~\ref{ch:ch4}--\ref{ch:ch7}; the principal findings are summarised in \S\ref{sec:ch1-contribution}. The parallel typologies of Chapters~\ref{ch:ch4}--\ref{ch:ch5}, the Hungarian-matched cross-country reading of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6}, and the robustness battery of Chapter~\ref{ch:ch7} jointly support the regime-as-structuring-institution claim, document the empirical case for the subjective block as constituent input, identify the country-specific archetypes that carry the bulk of the comparative content, and develop the operational implications for the silver-economy market structure that the typological reading delivers.

\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:ch1-contribution}

Four contributions structure the analytical and operational case of the thesis.

The first is the typological identification of the Isolation Paradox in the Italian over-65 population: a 26.9\% segment of the analytical sample (Moderate Isolated, the largest single cluster; Chapter~\ref{ch:ch4}) that is functionally and economically autonomous but socially and digitally disconnected, and that under-utilises the discretionary, navigation-intensive segments of the formal healthcare system. Regression-adjusted analysis controlling for age, chronic conditions, self-rated health, income, gender, and depressive symptoms returns a 0.67 fewer specialist contacts per year ($p < 0.001$) and a 28-percentage-point lower preventive dental-care attendance rate ($p < 0.001$) for Moderate Isolated relative to the structurally comparable Traditional Social cluster (\S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-healthcare}). The configuration has no structural counterpart in the Swedish typology and is invisible to the existing operational categories of the Italian welfare regime. The Isolation Paradox is the headline empirical finding of the thesis and the principal substantive case for product-design and policy innovation in the Italian silver economy.

The second contribution is the typological extension of the comparative welfare-regime literature: the documented regime contrast operates not only on aggregate outcomes but on the structural shape of the late-life partition that the same multidimensional segmentation pipeline returns from each population. The contrast operates jointly on three latent dimensions of the typology --- the binary versus graduated structure of fragility, the operational meaning of the digital channel as substitute or complement of the institutional infrastructure, and the late-life trajectory of the high-resource pole --- in a coherent pattern that the thesis reads as the typological signature of the regime.

The third contribution is methodological: the empirical case for treating the subjective dimensions of late-life experience as constituent inputs to the segmentation rather than as external validation outcomes. Two findings support the case. First, the 4D specification that drops the subjective block returns a materially lower external discriminative power on life satisfaction in both countries (\S\ref{sec:ch3-subjective-empirical}). Second, the qualitative contrast between Fragile Resigned and Fragile Depressed collapses under the same specification, eliminating the affective distinction that drives the differential policy responses developed in \S\ref{sec:ch4-isolation-paradox}.

The fourth contribution is operational: the explicit framing of the silver-economy design problem as a regime-conditioned typological problem. The three unmatched profiles --- Moderate Isolated in Italy, Asset Rich and Wealthy Digital in Sweden --- carry the country-specific signal in the standardised analytical space and therefore identify the empirical anchors of the design space, the segments on which product strategies cannot be transferred across regimes. The cross-country dental-care gap on the four matched pairs delivers, in turn, an order-of-magnitude indication of the preventive-care under-engagement revealed by the Swedish reference.

\section{Structure of the thesis}
\label{sec:ch1-roadmap}

The thesis is organised in eight chapters. Chapter~\ref{ch:ch2} reviews the five strands of literature that anchor the analytical strategy --- comparative welfare regimes, gerontological subjective dimensions, segmentation methodology, silver economy and gerontechnology, and European typological studies. Chapter~\ref{ch:ch3} sets out the analytical pipeline: SHARE Wave~9 sample preparation, factor-analytic preprocessing, K-means clustering ($k = 5$ Italy, $k = 6$ Sweden), Latent Class Analysis triangulation, and the empirical justification of the subjective block as constituent input.

Chapters~\ref{ch:ch4} and~\ref{ch:ch5} report the parallel typologies: Chapter~\ref{ch:ch4} develops the Italian typology of five archetypes and identifies the Isolation Paradox as the country-specific finding, while Chapter~\ref{ch:ch5} develops the Swedish typology of six archetypes and the three structural contrasts that distinguish it from the Italian one. Chapter~\ref{ch:ch6} integrates the two through the Hungarian-matched cross-country reading, the regime-signature interpretation of the three contrasts, the healthcare-utilisation comparison across matched pairs, and the implications for the silver-economy market structure. Chapter~\ref{ch:ch7} reports the robustness battery --- imputation sensitivity, internal-validity indices, external validation, cross-method convergence, and cross-wave panel stability between SHARE Wave~8 and Wave~9 --- and Chapter~\ref{ch:ch8} synthesises the findings, articulates the theoretical and operational contributions, documents the limitations, and identifies the directions of future research.

Chapter~\ref{ch:ch2} follows with the literature review and theoretical framework.
