\chapter{Ageing, Digitalisation, and the Problem of Invisible Vulnerability}
\label{ch:ch1}

\section{The Demographic Transformation of Europe}
Europe is ageing at an unprecedented rate. According to Eurostat projections, the share of the population aged 65 and over across the EU-27 is expected to rise from 21\% in 2023 to approximately 30\% by 2050, with the old-age dependency ratio---the number of individuals aged 65+ per 100 persons of working age---projected to increase from 33 to over 52. Italy occupies a particularly acute position within this transformation: with 23.8\% of its population aged 65 and over in 2024, it ranks as the second-oldest country in the EU after Germany, and its old-age dependency ratio of 37.5 is among the highest in the world.

The implications of this demographic shift extend well beyond pension sustainability. As populations age, the demand for healthcare, long-term care, and social services increases in both volume and complexity. Chronic multimorbidity becomes the norm rather than the exception: among Europeans aged 65 and over, more than 50\% report at least two chronic conditions, with the prevalence rising sharply with age. Cognitive decline, functional limitations, depression, and social isolation emerge as interrelated dimensions of vulnerability that require integrated, person-centred responses rather than disease-specific interventions.

Yet the systems designed to address these needs---healthcare, social care, and public administration---continue to operate largely on the basis of diagnostic categories and administrative classifications. An elderly individual enters the system when they receive a diagnosis, present at a hospital, or request a specific service. The system sees the disease, the hospitalisation, or the application; it does not see the person's cognitive trajectory, subjective well-being, social network, digital competence, or perception of their own future. This chapter argues that this representational gap---the inability of current systems to see the multidimensional, subjective reality of elderly individuals---constitutes a critical obstacle to effective and equitable care, and that the ongoing digitalisation of public services risks widening rather than narrowing this gap.

\section{Italy's Elderly Population: Between Family and State}
Italy's approach to elderly care is deeply shaped by its familialistic welfare tradition. Unlike the Scandinavian universalistic model, in which the state provides extensive formal services for the elderly---home care, senior centres, institutional support for independent living---the Italian model assigns the family a central role in care provision (Saraceno, 2010; Ferrera, 1996). The practical implications are significant: in Italy, informal caregiving by family members remains the dominant mode of elderly support, with daughters and daughters-in-law bearing a disproportionate share of the care burden. Public expenditure on long-term care as a share of GDP is among the lowest in Western Europe, and access to residential care (RSA) is rationed through long waiting lists and means-tested eligibility.

This model has functioned, with acknowledged strains, for decades. But several concurrent trends are eroding its viability. Declining fertility rates (Italy's total fertility rate of 1.24 in 2023 is among the lowest globally) reduce the pool of potential family caregivers. Increasing female labour force participation diminishes the availability of the traditional primary caregiver. Internal and external migration patterns alter the geographic proximity between elderly parents and adult children. And the progressive ageing of the oldest old (the population aged 85+, projected to double by 2050) increases the intensity and duration of care needs beyond what informal networks can sustain.

The result is a growing population of elderly individuals who fall between the cracks of the familialistic model: too independent to qualify for institutional care, too isolated or too frail to access services autonomously, and invisible to a system that activates only when a crisis occurs---a fall, a hospitalisation, a dementia diagnosis. Understanding who these individuals are, how many they are, and what characterises them is the first step toward addressing a problem that will only intensify in the coming decades.

\section{The Digitalisation of Public Services: Opportunity and Risk}
Against this demographic backdrop, Italy has embarked on an ambitious programme of digital transformation of its public services, with healthcare at the centre. The National Recovery and Resilience Plan (Piano Nazionale di Ripresa e Resilienza, PNRR), financed by the European Union's Next Generation EU programme, allocates €15.63 billion to Mission 6 (Health), with key investments in telemedicine infrastructure, the mandatory Electronic Health Record (Fascicolo Sanitario Elettronico, FSE), digital prescriptions, and online booking systems for medical services. The implicit assumption underlying this programme is that digital channels will improve access, reduce costs, and enhance the quality of care for all citizens.

For the elderly population, however, this assumption is problematic. The digital divide in Italy is one of the widest in the EU: according to the DESI (Digital Economy and Society Index) 2022, Italy ranks 18th out of 27 EU member states on basic digital skills, with particularly low scores among the population aged 65--74 (only 28\% report basic or above-basic digital skills, compared with 74\% in Sweden, 68\% in the Netherlands, and 58\% in Germany). The introduction of SPID (Sistema Pubblico di Identità Digitale) as the primary authentication mechanism for public services, while representing a significant modernisation, creates a de facto barrier for elderly individuals who do not possess the digital competence, the devices, or the cognitive capacity to navigate these systems.

The risk, therefore, is that the digitalisation of healthcare---conceived as a universalistic improvement---may in practice function as a mechanism of selective exclusion, disproportionately affecting those who need services most: the elderly, the cognitively impaired, the socially isolated, and the economically disadvantaged. Understanding which segments of the elderly population are reachable through digital channels and which are not is essential for designing complementary access strategies that prevent the digital transformation from becoming a source of inequality rather than equity. The point is worth restating, because it is sometimes missed in the enthusiasm for digital modernisation. An infrastructure built for the reachable makes the unreachable invisible.

\section{What Current Systems Do Not See}
The information systems that govern Italian healthcare and social services---regional health information systems (SISS), hospital discharge databases (SDO), prescription registries, and exemption databases---are rich in clinical and administrative data. They record diagnoses, procedures, medications, hospitalisations, and exemption codes with considerable precision. For the elderly population, these systems can identify individuals with diabetes, heart failure, or dementia; they can flag frequent emergency department users and track pharmaceutical expenditure.

What these systems cannot capture is the multidimensional, subjective experience of ageing. They do not record whether an individual feels lonely, whether they have hope for the future, whether they can use the internet, whether their social network has contracted since the death of a spouse, or whether they perceive their quality of life as satisfactory despite (or because of) their medical conditions. Yet these dimensions---social connectivity, subjective well-being, cognitive functioning, digital accessibility---are increasingly recognised as determinants of health trajectories that are at least as consequential as clinical diagnoses (Courtin \& Knapp, 2017; Holt-Lunstad et al., 2015; Stern, 2002).

Consider two elderly women, both aged 78, both with two chronic conditions and a recent widow's bereavement. To the health information system, they are identical: same age category, same chronic disease burden, same marital status change. But one has a supportive daughter living nearby, uses a smartphone, attends a weekly choir, and looks forward to each day. The other lives alone, has never used the internet, has no regular social contacts outside of medical appointments, and reports no hope for the future. Their clinical profiles are the same; their trajectories are radically different. The first is on a path of resilient ageing; the second is on a path toward frailty, institutionalisation, and, statistically, earlier mortality. The current system sees neither the difference nor the trajectory.

\section{The Opportunity: SHARE as a Comparative Laboratory}
The Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) provides a unique opportunity to address this information gap. As a harmonised, cross-national survey administered to over 150,000 individuals aged 50 and over across 28 European countries, SHARE captures precisely the dimensions that administrative systems miss: self-perceived health, cognitive performance (verbal fluency, memory, orientation), social network size and structure, digital competence, depressive symptoms, perceived quality of life (CASP-12), loneliness (UCLA scale), hope for the future, and subjective survival expectations. Critically, these variables are measured using identical instruments across countries, enabling valid cross-national comparisons.

The comparison between Italy and Sweden is particularly instructive. Sweden's universalistic welfare model, with its extensive public support for independent living in old age, represents the institutional antipode of Italy's familialistic approach. If a multidimensional segmentation of the elderly population reveals similar profile structures in both countries but different levels of subjective well-being, social connectivity, and digital engagement, the differences can be attributed to the institutional context rather than to individual characteristics. Such a finding would transform the policy discussion from `these are the characteristics of vulnerable elderly' to `these are the systemic conditions that produce vulnerable elderly'---a fundamentally different and more actionable framing.

\section{Research Questions}
This thesis addresses three interrelated research questions:

\textbf{RQ1.} What distinct profiles emerge within the Italian elderly population when segmented across five dimensions---health, economic resources, digital and social accessibility, cognitive functioning, and subjective perception---and do these profiles include segments that are invisible to current monitoring systems?

\textbf{RQ2.} Does the same segmentation framework, applied to the Swedish elderly population, produce comparable profile structures? And if so, do the matched profiles exhibit different levels of subjective well-being, social connectivity, and digital engagement?

\textbf{RQ3.} What does the cross-country comparison reveal about the role of institutional and contextual factors in shaping the experience of ageing, and what implications does it carry for the design of elderly-oriented public services in Italy?

The first question is addressed in Chapter 4, which presents the Italian segmentation results and identifies five distinct profiles, including a Moderate Isolated segment comprising 28.5\% of the sample---a group characterised by adequate objective conditions but extreme social isolation. The second question is addressed in Chapter 5, which applies the identical analytical framework to Sweden and documents a systematic gap of approximately +5 CASP points and +8 verbal fluency points in favour of Swedish elderly across all matched profiles, as well as the absence in Sweden of a profile equivalent to the Italian Moderate Isolated. The third question is addressed in Chapters 7 and 8, which demonstrate the healthcare-utilization consequences of profile membership and draw implications for Italian social and health policy in the context of the ongoing digital transformation.

\section{Contributions and Structure of the Thesis}
This thesis makes three principal contributions to the existing literature. First, it introduces subjective perception variables---loneliness, perceived quality of life (CASP-12), hope for the future, interest in things, and survival expectations---as \emph{inputs} to the segmentation, rather than as validation outcomes. This represents a departure from the existing SHARE segmentation literature, which has relied exclusively on objective indicators (Lestari et al., 2022; Rodrigues et al., 2023; Han et al., 2025).

Second, it provides a cross-country comparison of elderly profiles between Italy and Sweden using identical variables and methods, enabling the isolation of institutional effects from individual characteristics. No prior study has conducted a five-dimensional, multidimensional segmentation comparison between a Southern European and a Northern European country using SHARE Wave 9 data.

Third, it employs Latent Class Analysis alongside PCA-based clustering and Factor Analysis, providing methodological triangulation that is not standard in the course curriculum. The convergence between distance-based and model-based approaches on the extreme profiles (Fragile and Connected) strengthens confidence in the identified segments, while the instability of the central mass is itself a substantive finding about the nature of ageing trajectories.

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the relevant literature on elderly segmentation, the digital divide in ageing populations, and the distinction between objective conditions and subjective well-being. Chapter 3 describes the data, variables, and analytical methods. Chapter 4 presents the Italian segmentation results. Chapter 5 presents the cross-country comparison. Chapter 6 examines methodological robustness and sensitivity. Chapter 7 analyses healthcare utilization patterns across profiles. Chapter 8 discusses implications and concludes.
